Game Design Document

Aquarium Anomaly

Night-shift aquarium job · anomaly horror-lite · Roblox · pre-production, greenlit for a trend-test build

You are the new night-shift worker at Blackwater Aquarium, and the job is real work. Every shift you have a checklist: scrub algae off the tank glass, feed the fish, fix the filter pump when it rattles, keep the water chemistry in the green. The problem is that something in this aquarium is wrong, and it changes every night. While your hands are busy with a sponge and your back is to the hall, a shark grows human teeth, a tank empties itself, a shape follows you on the CCTV. Spot the anomaly and report it before the timer runs out, keep the aquarium running, survive to 6 AM, and get paid for both jobs. It is Papers Please meets FNAF in a flooded blue building: an anomaly game where you are a worker with a full task list, not a tourist with a flashlight, and the horror hits hardest exactly when you are too busy to look.

01Design Pillars

A worker, not a watcherThe player has a real job to do every shift. Anomaly-spotting sits on top of active upkeep work, the way Animal Hospital layers it on top of running a clinic. Being busy is what makes the scares land.
"Wait, was that always there?"Every anomaly should make the player second-guess themselves. The tension comes from not being sure. The player's own memory is a core mechanic.
One thumb, 90 seconds to funA new mobile player should understand the game and find their first anomaly inside 90 seconds. No long tutorial.
Content is data, not new mapsAnomalies and most tasks are rows in a table. Adding ten more after launch should take under a day. That is what keeps the game cheap to run.
Made to be filmedEvery rare anomaly is a possible clip. The reveals are set up for a camera, with one signature creature people put on thumbnails.

02Core Gameplay Loop

A shift now runs two jobs in the same window: the anomaly patrol (walk the tanks, spot what is wrong, report it) and shift tasks (aquarium upkeep, covered in §3). The two are meant to fight for your attention. What follows is the patrol half. The task half and how they interleave are in §3.

Moment to moment (the 30 second loop)

  1. Walk to the next tank on the route. Tanks are numbered T1 to T8 around one loop corridor.
  2. Observe. Compare the tank to how it normally looks. Players learn the baselines through play, and a "Reference Tablet" tool shows a photo of the normal state for anyone who buys or earns it.
  3. Decide. Looks normal, move on. Looks off, open the Report screen.
  4. Report. Aim the reticle at the tank, tap Report, pick one of four big buttons: Creature, Tank, Environment, Entity.
  5. Resolve. A correct report plays an "ANOMALY CONTAINED" sound, pays out, and the anomaly reverts or gets escorted out. A wrong report gives a strike, a short alarm, and a small pay cut.

How one shift runs

Win: report enough of the live anomalies (see §5) before the timer ends and keep the aquarium running. Lose: take 3 strikes in one shift, or miss too many anomalies, and you get the "TERMINATED" screen. That screen is a clip in itself, since the signature creature shows up behind the player as it plays.

Session and long-term loops

Session, about 10 to 20 minutes. Play a few shifts, spend earnings in the break-room shop, try the next night tier, hit a wall or a scare, run one more shift.

Long-term, over days. Unlock night tiers (1 to 15 and up at launch), fill the Anomaly Log with a page for every anomaly you have seen, chase the daily bonus shift, and show off flashlight and uniform cosmetics in the lobby.

03Shift Tasks (the active-work layer)

Every night has two jobs running in the same window: the anomaly patrol from §2, and Shift Tasks, a rotating checklist of aquarium upkeep. Tasks appear on a small clipboard on the HUD with soft deadlines. Doing them earns Credits and keeps your shift rating up. Skipping them costs pay and, at higher tiers, triggers a consequence (a broken filter fogs a tank, which makes anomalies harder to spot).

The design rule for every task: it must either occupy your hands, fix your gaze, or move you somewhere specific. That is what makes the anomaly layer scarier, because the game can schedule anomalies against your attention instead of only against a timer.

How tasks and anomalies work together

The anomaly director (the system that picks and schedules anomalies each night) gets one new input: the player's current task state. In practice:

  • Hands-busy spawning. The director prefers to activate an anomaly in an opposite or adjacent tank while the player is mid-scrub or mid-dial. You hear a splash behind you during the chemistry hold and have to choose: break the hold and lose progress, or finish and hope you were not too slow.
  • Back-turn spawning. Filter repair and the supply room put the player in dead-end rooms. Some anomalies, Curator sightings especially, only trigger while the player is out of the hall, so the hall you come back to is not the one you left.
  • Task-shaped anomalies. A few catalog entries disguise themselves as tasks: an algae patch that regrows the second you finish, a feed hatch already open, a puddle that is warm. The checklist itself cannot be fully trusted, which is the Papers Please nerve the sub-genre runs on.
  • Fake-outs that protect the report economy. Failed upkeep produces anomaly look-alikes: belly-up fish from bad chemistry, fog from a dead filter, frantic unfed fish. Reporting these wastes a report. Good workers are better spotters because their baseline is clean, which is the loop-level reason the two systems belong together.
  • Pacing control. Tasks give the director a dial it never had. Quiet nights can be work-heavy and anomaly-light. Nightmare tiers can stack a filter failure, a feeding round, and a Curator event into the same three minutes.

Per-shift load: 4 to 6 tasks on Night 1, rising to 8 to 10 by Night 10 and up, tuned so a competent player is busy about 60 percent of the shift and watching tanks the other 40 percent.

04The Anomaly System

How it works under the hood

Every anomaly is one entry in a table (AnomalyCatalog), read by a server-side AnomalyService:

{
  id = "SHARK_TEETH",
  name = "Denture Danger",
  category = "Creature",          -- Creature, Tank, Environment, Entity
  locations = {"T3"},             -- which tank(s) it can occur in
  weight = 10,                    -- spawn weighting within its difficulty band
  minNight = 2,                   -- earliest night it can appear
  difficulty = 2,                 -- 1 easy to 5 hardest
  applyFn = "SwapMeshVariant",    -- named handler: swap mesh, texture, toggle, script
  params = { variant = "SharkHumanTeeth" },
  clipTag = false,                -- true = rare, staged for filming
}
  • Shared handlers, not one script per anomaly. About eight handlers cover the whole catalogue: SwapMeshVariant, SwapTexture, ToggleVisibility, PlayAnimOverride, MoveModel, LightingOverride, SoundEmit, SpawnEntity. A new anomaly is usually one table row plus one variant asset.
  • Spawn table. At the start of a shift, AnomalyService picks N anomalies (N grows by night), filters by minNight, weights by weight, avoids putting two in the same tank, and keeps a spread of difficulty so a shift is never all easy or all hard. Some slots are set to fire 60 to 180 seconds in.
  • Task-aware scheduling. The director also reads the player's current task state (see §3), so it can time an anomaly for the moment your hands are full or your back is turned. This is one small hook, not a new system.
  • Report check. The client raycasts from the reticle to find the tank, then fires a RemoteEvent with the tank id and category. The server checks everything: is there a live anomaly in that tank, does the category match, has it already been reported. A matching category pays an extra 25 percent. The right tank with the wrong category still counts as found, so it stays forgiving for younger players while rewarding the ones who learn the categories.
  • False reports. Reporting a clean tank costs one strike and a small pay cut, with a mocking announcement over the PA ("Attention staff: the fish are fine."). Strikes reset each shift, so reporting stays a real decision. Reporting all eight tanks on purpose always loses.
  • Difficulty budget. Each night has a point budget, so Night 6 might be 11 points made of one 4, one 3, and two 2s. All of it tunes from one config table.

  • Two interleaved tracks. The patrol track can end your shift (3 strikes, or too many missed anomalies). The task track cannot fail the shift on its own. An ignored task ticks down to a consequence (a fogged tank, erratic fish, a slip hazard, a pay cut) that makes the patrol track harder. Anomalies can kill your shift, tasks can only make it harder and poorer, so the player always knows which fire to fight first.
  • Strikes. Three false reports end the shift, and you keep 25 percent of the pay. There is no sanity meter at launch (see the note below). The feeling of losing your grip comes from the lights, the audio, and the task consequences degrading what you can see.
  • Failure does not set you back. Failing never lowers your night tier. You retry the same night. It costs time, not progress, which matters for keeping younger players.
  • Grades. S means every anomaly found, every category right, every task done, no strikes. Grades only gate the Overtime leaderboard, but the results screen makes an S feel earned with a gold stamp and its own sound.
On a sanity meter. We keep 3-strikes as the only fail state for launch. Two fail meters is a lot for a new player in a 5 minute shift, and Animal Hospital earns its sanity meter with a much longer session. The task-consequence system already does most of what a sanity meter would do, which is degrade the player's ability to perceive. A Composure meter (drained by Curator proximity and witnessed anomalies, restored at a break-room coffee machine, unlocking at Night 8 and up) is designed and priced in §12 as a clean post-launch addition, so the door stays open once retention data justifies it.

06Economy and Progression

Currency: Credits (₡), one soft currency. No second currency at this scope.

How pay splits now that there are two jobs

Each night still pays out a set total, but that total is now split so tasks carry real weight:

ComponentShare of the night's payNotes
Base shift completion50%Survive to 6 AM.
Anomaly reports30%Per correct report, as before.
Task completion20%Per task, prorated. Late tasks pay half.
Perfect Shift bonus+25% on topAll anomalies caught, all tasks done, no false reports. Replaces the old flat perfect bonus.

Base pay per night stays where it was: Night 1 to 2 pays ₡50, 3 to 5 pays ₡90, 6 to 8 pays ₡140, 9 to 11 pays ₡200, 12 and up pays ₡275. Split applies on top. A ₡140 night becomes about ₡70 base, up to ₡42 from reports, up to ₡28 from tasks, and a Perfect Shift near ₡175. Total Credits entering the economy per night stays within about 5 percent of the old numbers, so tool prices, gamepass value, and tier gating do not need rebalancing. Task pay is capped, never bonused above the cap, which stops task-farming inflation.

07Monetization

At about 26 to 28 percent net after Roblox's cut and DevEx, ₨1,000 of player spend is worth roughly $3.50 to us. Now that the build costs more (see §12), the break-even point is higher too. A $1,600 build pays for itself at around ₨460,000 of gross spend, which needs stronger retention or more visits than the old cheap build did. That is what the kill or scale gate in §14 tests.

Guardrails. No loot boxes, no gacha, no countdown timers on purchases, and no purchase prompts during a scare or on the termination screen. Second Chance is offered once, calmly, with a decline button the same size as the buy button. All prices show before any confirm, and nothing paid blocks seeing the content. Money buys speed and status, never the only way through. That is the right call for a young audience, and it is also the safe call, since Roblox moderates predatory patterns more each year.

08Retention and Sharing

Reasons to come back each day

  • Double pay on the first shift of the day, plus a Daily Anomaly, one hand-picked anomaly guaranteed to appear that day. Players compare notes on it ("did you find today's?").
  • A login streak. Five days in a row earns an exclusive flashlight skin. It is cosmetic only, so breaking the streak does not sting.
  • The Anomaly Log fills only when you actually see an anomaly, and the rare ones are rare, so there is a long tail to chase.

Built for clips

  • The Curator, the signature creature, is made for thumbnails: pale, too tall, in a soaked staff uniform, face never fully shown. It appears in only three set situations, and being rare is what makes it worth filming.
  • The rarest anomalies (#20 Mimic Plaque, #30 Name Tag) use the player's own avatar or username, which is the most shareable trick in the genre ("it knew my name").
  • Task-shaped anomalies are their own clip engine: the algae you scoop that turns and looks at you, the dead fish that twitches. These happen while the player is calm and working, so the reaction is bigger.
  • The update schedule is also the marketing. A "5 NEW ANOMALIES" badge on the icon every week or two is cheap to back up, because the content is data (§4).

09UI and UX

Seven screens, kept small on purpose:

  1. Lobby and main menu. An elevator hub with Play (big), Shop, Anomaly Log, Settings, and the gamepass shelf. Other players are visible, which gives cosmetics a place to show.
  2. Shift HUD. Timer at top center, an anomalies-found counter ("2/4", shown on Nights 1 to 5 to teach, hidden from Night 6 on for dread), a small task clipboard with the current checklist, three strike pips, a two-button tool belt in the corners, and the reticle.
  3. Report screen. A dimmed full screen with four big category buttons and Cancel. One tap to open, one to send. No typing at any point.
  4. Results screen. Grade stamp, a line-by-line pay breakdown (base, reports, tasks, bonus), Next Shift and Lobby buttons.
  5. Shop (break room). A tools tab, a cosmetics tab, and a Robux tab, in that order, Robux last.
  6. Anomaly Log. A grid of cards, with a "?" silhouette for the ones not yet found.
  7. Settings. Volume, camera sensitivity, a reduced-flash toggle (needed because of the flicker anomalies), and graphics quality.

Mobile first. Default one-thumb movement with auto camera. Both the Report button and the task interact button are large, live in the thumb zone, and only appear in context (over a tank, or next to a task), so there is no clutter. Interact range is generous, about 12 studs, and tap targets are at least 44 points. On PC, E interacts and reports, F is the flashlight. Console uses ContextActionService.

10Art and Audio

Look. Low poly, with lighting doing most of the work. Flat-shaded models with chunky shapes that read on a five-inch screen, Future lighting where the budget allows and a Voxel fallback for weaker phones. The palette is deep teal-black (#0A1A22), tank-glow cyan (#38D6E0), and a warning amber (#E8A13C) for the interface, with one color held back: anomaly red (#C7303A), used only for a confirmed anomaly so it keeps its meaning.

Asset list, kept short. One aquarium interior (a loop corridor, a lobby, a break room, a security office, a filter room, a supply room, a catwalk over the tanks, and about eight tank alcoves), eight tank sets with baseline creatures, roughly 25 anomaly variant meshes and textures (mostly recolors and mesh swaps of the baselines), the task props (sponge, mop, feed bucket, net pole, filter cartridge, dial panel), The Curator model, one staff uniform plus three recolors, and the flashlight, tablet, and scanner props. A lot of the fish are free marketplace meshes retextured to fit, which is fine at this budget and worth stating plainly.

Audio carries the horror, and it is the cheapest scare per dollar. A low tank hum runs under everything, each zone has its own bubbler loop (and one anomaly, #15, is the absence of it), a muffled whale-song layer sits far off, a heartbeat comes in under 60 seconds, and the "ANOMALY CONTAINED" sound has to feel good, since it is the game's reward chime. The task sounds do double duty: a scrub, a pour, a valve creak, and the filter rattle that calls you into the one room with no view. Add three Curator sounds and the glass knocks. All of it comes from Roblox's licensed library and CC0 sources, so the cost stays near zero.

11Technical Architecture

  • The server owns anything that matters. AnomalyService on the server holds the spawn table, the live anomaly state, and the checks. Clients never learn which anomalies are active. The visual changes replicate, but the truth stays on the server. A report is a RemoteEvent with tank id and category, and the server checks the player is close enough (within 20 studs), limits reports to one per second, and works out the pay. A determined exploiter could compare what is rendered against cached baselines, which we accept at this scope, since the only thing at stake is soft currency.
  • Task system. A TaskService on the server schedules tasks, tracks completion and deadlines, and fires the consequence hooks (fog a tank, spawn a puddle, dock pay). It shares the same server-authority and rate-limit approach as reports. Task state is one input to the anomaly director.
  • Saving. ProfileService, a session-locked wrapper over DataStore, holds credits, night progress, tools, log entries, and streaks. It is the standard way to avoid duplication exploits from rejoin races. One versioned schema, with a migration stub in from day one.
  • Structure. One place. Each player runs their own shift state, and the map is shared but anomaly and task visuals are sent only to that player. This avoids the cost of reserved servers and keeps the lobby social. Two players at the same tank can see different things, which in this genre is a feature and a running joke.
  • Mobile performance. Streaming on, a budget of about 8,000 parts in view, everything anchored so physics is near zero, fish swimming through TweenService rather than physics, one texture atlas per tank, and a target of 30 fps on a 2019-era Android phone. Voxel lighting kicks in on low quality.
  • Services used. DataStoreService through ProfileService, RemoteEvent and RemoteFunction (few, all checked), CollectionService for tagging tank zones and task points, TweenService, SoundService groups, MarketplaceService with server-side receipt handling and an idempotent grant log, and AnalyticsService for custom events (shift_start, shift_end, report, task_done, purchase, night_unlocked).

Minimum viable launch versus the full polished game

MVP floor: about 112 hours, $1,344, call it $1,350 to $1,600 with overrun. Baseline 82 hours plus only the task framework (N1), algae cleaning (N2), mopping (N3), feeding (N4), water chemistry (N6), the economy update (N10), and a trimmed 5 hours of balancing. This ships a real active-work game: the anchor task, two hands-busy tasks, one gaze-lock minigame, and task pay. It cuts the filter room, Skimmer Duty, Reading Log, and the fancy director integration. Anomalies still spawn on their own schedule and just happen to catch you working, which is about 70 percent of the effect for none of N9's cost.

Full polished game: about 176 hours, $2,100 likely. Everything in the table above, including director integration, all seven tasks, and full balancing.

Full game plus the Animal Hospital feature set (sanity and defense): about 199 hours, $2,150 to $2,650. Add O1 and O2. Skip O3 (co-op) for launch. It is the single most expensive and riskiest line, and the genre does not require it.

What is cheap, what is expensive, where to cut

Cheap wins, high tension per dollar: algae cleaning, mopping, feeding, Reading Log, Skimmer Duty. These are decals, tools, prompts, and timers, roughly $36 to $72 each.

Real cost centers: the filter room minigame ($96, needs a new room and a state machine), director integration ($60 plus most of the rework buffer), sanity ($156), defense ($120), and co-op ($300, and it destabilizes everything else).

Cut ladder to hit a lower budget, in order:

  1. Cut co-op (O3), saves $300. Recommended no matter what.
  2. Cut defense (O2) and sanity (O1), saves $276. Ship 3-strikes only, add these later.
  3. Cut director integration (N9) and half the rework buffer, saves about $96. Anomalies no longer aim at your busy hands, they just coexist with them. Fine at launch.
  4. Cut filter room (N5), Skimmer (N7), Reading Log (N8), saves $192. This is the MVP floor.
  5. Below that you are back to the observation game, which is the design we are moving away from. Do not cut past step 4.

16Reuse for the Fishing Game

Part of this project is research we buy toward the larger fishing game. What carries over directly:

  • Creature library. All eight baseline rigs and the swim-tween system become catchable fish, with no rework beyond stat tables.
  • Water and tank kit. Water materials, caustics lighting, and bubbler particles feed ocean and harbor scenes.
  • AnomalyService and TaskService patterns. The content-as-data catalog, the generic handlers, and the task scheduler generalize to fish species, rare catches, weather events, and dock chores. This is the most valuable thing the project produces.
  • The task tools. Hold-to-clean, fetch-and-deliver, and dial minigames are exactly the kind of active work a fishing game uses for bait prep, boat upkeep, and selling the catch.
  • ProfileService schema and receipt handling. A drop-in economy backbone.
  • The Curator and the anomaly idea. A rare "anomalous catch" event in the fishing game ("you caught something wrong") is a built-in crossover, and each game can promote the other in its lobby.
  • Audio bed. The underwater ambience library moves over whole.
  • What we learn. Real numbers on mobile horror-lite retention, how the 79 to 249 ₨ price points perform, whether the active-work layer lifts session length, and which anomaly categories drive clips. All of it feeds the fishing game's design.

Because the active-work layer adds reusable interaction systems on top of the reusable creatures and water, a larger share now carries forward, roughly 40 to 55 of the 147 build hours. Even a game we shut down returns a solid chunk of its cost to the next one.
